# Editorial Standard

## Purpose

This file defines the standard structure, tone, workflow, and publication requirements for reader-facing content.

The product is designed to provoke curiosity, reveal intellectual depth, and encourage exploration of authentic Indic textual traditions.

## Core reader

Primary audience:

A culturally curious Indian professional who:
- does not need to know Sanskrit;
- is familiar with some traditions through cultural references;
- wants to understand what the texts actually say;
- is interested in meaningful modern connections;
- values intellectual depth without academic friction.

## Desired reader response

The ideal reaction is:

- "They were asking this?"
- "They were actually doing this?"
- "I thought I knew this, but I didn't."
- "They disagreed about this?"

The emotional target is:

curiosity mixed with intellectual awe.

## Daily entry model

A daily entry should normally follow:

1. DISCOVER
2. ORIGINAL
3. UNDERSTAND
4. PERSPECTIVES
5. CONNECT
6. EXPLORE

Not every entry must force all six sections if one is genuinely unnecessary.

## 1. DISCOVER

Purpose:
Create curiosity before explanation.

Normally begin with:
- a question;
- a puzzle;
- an intellectual tension;
- a surprising observation.

Prefer:

"How do you know that you know?"

over:

"Today's verse is Nyaya Sutra 1.1.3."

The hook must not overstate the source.

## 2. ORIGINAL

Include where applicable:

- original-language text;
- transliteration;
- exact source name;
- chapter/section/verse/sutra/mantra/karika reference;
- short source note;
- audio link when available.

For long passages, show only the relevant excerpt and link to the full passage.

The original text should have visual and intellectual space before modern interpretation begins.

## 3. UNDERSTAND

Explain:

- literal or close meaning;
- important technical terms;
- immediate textual context;
- the central intellectual idea;
- what a simplistic translation would miss.

Target default length:
approximately 100-180 words.

Use simple modern English without flattening technical ideas.

## 4. PERSPECTIVES

Explain how the passage or concept has been interpreted.

May include:
- one major traditional commentator;
- multiple commentarial traditions;
- later intellectual development;
- disagreement between schools;
- a note that interpretation is relatively straightforward.

Do not manufacture disagreement merely for variety.

Never present one school's reading as the universal Indic interpretation when material disagreement exists.

## 5. CONNECT

Ask:

"Why might this still be interesting today?"

Every connection must use an approved relationship type from `CLAIM_POLICY.md`.

Reader-facing labels may include:

- Still the Same Question
- Conceptual Parallel
- Historical Connection
- Historical Continuity
- Scientific Correspondence
- Modern Interpretation
- Contested Connection
- Not the Same Thing

The reader-facing prose must explain:
- what the ancient source says;
- what the modern comparison says;
- why the comparison is interesting;
- where the comparison stops.

A modern connection is optional.

Never invent one merely to make the entry feel relevant.

## 6. EXPLORE

Provide approximately 3-5 rabbit holes.

Prefer different types:

### Read
Surrounding verses, chapter, or source passage.

### Understand
A technical Sanskrit or conceptual term.

### Perspective
A commentator or competing school.

### Compare
Another civilisation, text, tradition, or historical approach.

### Modern
A paper, book, scientific topic, philosophical question, or historical study.

### Listen
Recitation, explanation, or longer commentary.

### See
A diagram, mathematical derivation, astronomical model, table, or visual explanation.

Deep dives should reuse existing pages where possible rather than creating duplicate explanations.

## Entry archetypes

Every daily entry may be classified under one or more archetypes:

- big-question
- argument
- method
- mathematical-insight
- observation-of-nature
- explanatory-model
- language-about-language
- mind-and-experience
- society-and-decision-making
- commonly-misunderstood-idea

These are editorial archetypes, not the primary public taxonomy.

## Surprise types

Use one or more:

- they-asked-this
- they-did-this
- i-misunderstood-this
- they-disagreed-about-this

## Reader-facing taxonomy

Do not rely only on academic disciplines.

Public pathways should often be framed as questions, for example:

- What is the self?
- How do we know?
- How does language mean?
- How does the world work?
- What makes life healthy?
- How should we act?
- How should society be organised?
- Why does art move us?
- How should we reason under uncertainty?

Underlying metadata may separately track:
- discipline;
- text;
- tradition;
- historical period;
- commentator;
- concept;
- modern connection;
- difficulty.

## Length

Daily entries should support a 3-5 minute default experience.

The main entry should normally be concise.

Depth belongs in linked:
- passages;
- concept pages;
- source pages;
- deep dives;
- pathways.

Do not turn every daily entry into an essay.

## Deep dives

A deep dive is a reusable knowledge object, not simply a longer daily entry.

A deep dive should:
- answer one clear question;
- link back to relevant primary passages;
- distinguish claim classes;
- expose evidence;
- link related concepts and entries;
- remain useful when reached independently.

Examples:

"What is pramana?"

"How did Panini's rule system work?"

"What does nirodha mean in Yoga Sutra 1.2?"

"How did Brahmagupta calculate with zero?"

## Daily entry versus passage

A passage is a source object.

An entry is an editorial experience.

Do not duplicate the canonical source text independently across many entries when a verified passage object can be referenced.

## Daily entry versus concept

A concept page should accumulate understanding across many texts.

Examples:
- atman;
- brahman;
- pramana;
- rasa;
- dharma;
- shunya;
- agni.

A daily entry may introduce the concept but should link to the canonical concept page for deeper treatment.

## Tone

Use:
- simple but intelligent English;
- short paragraphs;
- precise claims;
- curiosity;
- restraint;
- respectful treatment of traditions;
- clear acknowledgement of uncertainty.

Avoid:
- devotional preaching unless explaining a devotional interpretation;
- academic jargon without explanation;
- clickbait;
- civilisational triumphalism;
- condescension toward ancient thinkers;
- condescension toward religious interpretations;
- forced productivity lessons;
- motivational quotation culture.

## Sanskrit

Assume the reader does not know Sanskrit.

Preserve Sanskrit where it matters.

Where a term is important:
1. show Devanagari when useful;
2. provide IAST transliteration;
3. explain the term simply;
4. avoid pretending one English word captures its entire semantic range.

Do not overload the reader with grammar unless the linguistic point itself is the subject.

## Audio

Where appropriate, an entry may link to:

- authentic recitation;
- slow pronunciation;
- English narration;
- short commentary;
- longer scholar or podcast-style explanation.

Prefer trained human reciters for:
- Vedic recitation;
- metrically or accentually sensitive material;
- canonical pronunciation recordings.

AI narration may be used for:
- English explanations;
- summaries;
- non-sensitive explanatory audio.

Audio provenance should be recorded.

## Citations

Every entry must make its evidence inspectable.

The reader-facing page should not become citation-heavy, but should provide:
- exact primary-text reference;
- source links;
- modern references where relevant;
- a path to detailed source information.

Claims should internally remain traceable to their evidence.

## Editorial workflow

Recommended lifecycle:

candidate
→ researching
→ source-verified
→ drafted
→ red-teamed
→ editor-approved
→ ready
→ scheduled
→ published
→ revised if necessary

No AI-generated entry may skip directly from draft to published.

## Candidate selection

A candidate should ideally satisfy at least one of:

- raises a profound question;
- shows a structured method;
- contains a genuine technical insight;
- reveals unexpected intellectual depth;
- challenges a popular misunderstanding;
- shows disagreement across traditions;
- offers a defensible modern connection;
- opens several worthwhile rabbit holes.

Reject candidates that are merely famous quotations without sufficient intellectual depth.

## Variety

Publishing should avoid long runs of similar material.

Balance over time across:
- Vedic literature;
- Upanishads;
- Vedangas;
- philosophy;
- mathematics and astronomy;
- medicine;
- grammar and language;
- aesthetics;
- statecraft;
- Dharmashastra;
- epics.

Also balance:
- spiritual/philosophical;
- technical;
- historical;
- linguistic;
- scientific;
- social;
- aesthetic material.

## Controversial material

Do not omit controversial material solely because it is controversial.

When publishing:
- quote accurately;
- explain context;
- distinguish prescription from historical description;
- identify interpretive disagreement;
- avoid modern endorsement unless explicitly supported;
- avoid using controversy as clickbait.

## Corrections

Substantive corrections should be versioned.

Reader-facing revision history should be available when:
- a translation materially changes;
- a source reference is corrected;
- an interpretation is revised;
- a modern scientific comparison is weakened or removed;
- a significant source is added that changes the entry.

Minor formatting and typographical corrections do not require public revision notes.

## Final publication test

Before approving an entry, ask:

1. Is the source authentic and exactly identified?
2. Is the translation defensible?
3. Is enough surrounding context understood?
4. Are technical terms treated carefully?
5. Are traditional interpretations attributed?
6. Are disagreements represented when material?
7. Is every modern claim properly sourced?
8. Is the relationship type correct?
9. Have we overstated historical influence?
10. Have we overstated scientific equivalence?
11. Would a specialist find the framing fair?
12. Would a general reader understand it?
13. Is there a genuine reason to be curious?
14. Does it lead naturally into deeper exploration?

If the entry is accurate but dull, improve the framing.

If the entry is exciting but unsupported, weaken or reject it.

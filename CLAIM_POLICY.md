# Claim Policy

## Purpose

This file defines how claims are classified, written, checked, and presented.

The project must never allow an attractive narrative to become stronger than its evidence.

## Claim classes

Every substantive claim should conceptually belong to one of these classes.

### TEXTUAL

Directly supported by the primary text.

Examples:
- what a verse says;
- what terms occur;
- what sequence of ideas appears;
- what a rule states.

### COMMENTARIAL

Supported by an identifiable traditional commentator or school.

Examples:
- Shankara interprets...
- Ramanuja reads...
- later Nyaya commentators argue...

### HISTORICAL

Supported by historical scholarship.

Examples:
- dating;
- authorship;
- transmission;
- institutional context;
- influence;
- intellectual priority;
- social practice.

### MODERN

Supported by contemporary scholarship, science, philosophy, or other modern evidence.

Examples:
- current neuroscience;
- modern astronomy;
- mathematical interpretation;
- contemporary philosophy;
- history of science.

### EDITORIAL

Our explanatory framing or interpretive bridge.

Examples:
- why an idea may still be interesting;
- how two intellectual problems can be compared;
- why a passage is surprising.

Editorial claims must never be presented as though they occur in the primary text.

### CONTESTED

A claim for which serious disagreement or material uncertainty exists.

The disagreement must be represented when relevant to the user's understanding.

## Strength rule

The wording of a claim must not be stronger than the evidence.

Prefer:

“offers an interesting parallel”

over:

“anticipated”

Prefer:

“describes”

over:

“discovered”

Prefer:

“has been compared with”

over:

“is equivalent to”

Prefer:

“may suggest”

over:

“proves”

when evidence is limited.

## Ancient-modern relationship types

Use one or more of the following:

### still-the-same-question

The ancient and modern traditions investigate a recognisably similar intellectual question.

No historical influence is implied.

### conceptual-parallel

There is a meaningful structural or conceptual similarity.

The theories, methods, or underlying assumptions may still differ substantially.

### historical-connection

There is evidence of contact, transmission, borrowing, response, or influence.

This requires historical evidence.

### historical-continuity

There is a traceable line of development within a tradition or across texts.

### scientific-correspondence

A specific ancient statement or model substantially corresponds with an accepted modern scientific or mathematical result.

Use sparingly.

The exact point of correspondence must be identified.

### modern-interpretation

A modern thinker, discipline, or editorial approach uses the ancient material as a lens for contemporary questions.

### contested-connection

A proposed ancient-modern relationship exists in scholarship or public discussion but remains disputed.

### not-the-same-thing

A popular equivalence is misleading enough that the distinction deserves explicit explanation.

## Scientific correspondence test

Before using `scientific-correspondence`, answer all of the following:

1. What exactly does the ancient source state?
2. Is the relevant meaning explicit rather than inserted through translation?
3. What exactly does modern science or mathematics establish?
4. What is the shared proposition?
5. Where does the ancient model differ?
6. Is there strong scholarly support for reading the ancient passage this way?
7. Are we confusing correct observation with correct theory?
8. Are we confusing resemblance with prediction?

If these questions cannot be answered clearly, use a weaker relationship type.

## Historical influence test

Never infer influence from chronological priority alone.

To claim influence, look for evidence such as:
- textual transmission;
- translation;
- known contact;
- citation;
- intellectual networks;
- terminology;
- documented reception;
- strong specialist historical scholarship.

“X came before Y” does not establish “X influenced Y.”

## Priority claims

Claims such as:
- first;
- earliest;
- invented;
- discovered;
- originated;
- centuries ahead;

require exceptional caution.

Prefer more precise language:

“one of the earliest surviving...”

“the text contains an early known formulation of...”

“the surviving evidence shows...”

Only use absolute priority language when the relevant historical scholarship is strong.

## Translation claims

A translation is itself interpretive.

When a claim depends on a Sanskrit term:

- retain the term where useful;
- identify plausible meanings;
- note when the chosen English term is approximate;
- avoid importing a modern technical meaning into an ancient word.

Examples of dangerous automatic equivalences:

- prana = oxygen
- agni = metabolism
- akasha = spacetime
- anu = modern atom
- citta = brain
- dosha = modern physiological system

Such comparisons may be explored, but equivalence must not be assumed.

## Traditional knowledge and modern validation

Ancient ideas do not require modern scientific validation to be intellectually valuable.

Do not force every entry into:
“ancient idea → modern science proves it.”

Valid modern connections may instead involve:
- philosophy;
- ethics;
- epistemology;
- systems thinking;
- history;
- linguistics;
- anthropology;
- mathematics;
- aesthetics;
- decision-making;
- conceptual comparison.

Some entries may have no justified modern scientific connection.

That is acceptable.

## Medicine

Never transform:

“Caraka states X”

into:

“X is medically effective”

without modern clinical evidence.

Clearly distinguish:
- historical theory;
- historical observation;
- traditional therapeutic claim;
- laboratory evidence;
- clinical evidence;
- current medical consensus.

## Mathematics

When presenting historical mathematics:

- reconstruct the exact rule;
- show an example where helpful;
- distinguish notation from concept;
- identify what matches modern mathematics;
- identify what does not;
- avoid retroactively assigning modern formalism where the source does not support it.

## Astronomy

Distinguish:
- observation;
- computational rule;
- predictive model;
- cosmology;
- coordinate/reference frame;
- modern scientific interpretation.

A correct observation does not imply an entirely correct cosmological model.

## Disagreement

When traditions or commentators materially disagree, do not collapse them into a synthetic “Indic view.”

Represent the disagreement clearly.

This applies especially to:
- self;
- Brahman;
- knowledge;
- language;
- causation;
- liberation;
- ontology;
- ritual;
- law;
- social order.

## Controversial texts

Do not exclude material merely because it is controversial.

For Dharmaśastra and other socially contested material:

- quote accurately;
- explain context;
- distinguish description from endorsement;
- identify later interpretation where relevant;
- avoid sanitising the text;
- avoid sensationalising the text.

## Red-team requirement

Before an entry is approved, ask:

- What sentence is most likely to overstate the evidence?
- What would a Sanskrit scholar object to?
- What would a historian object to?
- What would a specialist in the modern discipline object to?
- Have we converted analogy into equivalence?
- Have we hidden contradictory evidence?
- Would a reasonable reader leave with a false impression?

If yes, revise before publication.

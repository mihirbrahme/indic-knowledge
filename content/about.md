# About

Indic Knowledge is a source-first editorial project on India's textual and
intellectual traditions.

It exists because these texts are quoted far more often than they are read. The
gap between what a passage says and what it is said to say is usually where the
interesting material is — and it is almost always more interesting than the
paraphrase.

## What an entry is

An entry is a short reading experience, three to five minutes long, built on one
verified passage. It opens with a question, shows the original text with a
literal translation, explains the idea in plain English, attributes any
interpretation to whoever actually holds it, and stops.

An entry is not the canonical record of the text. That lives on the passage
page, which carries the original, the transliteration, the working translation,
the locator, and the sources it was read from.

## How sources are handled

The project distinguishes between a source being _registered_ and a source being
_read_. That difference is recorded on every passage.

<details>
<summary>How a passage is sourced</summary>

Each passage records:

- the **canonical text**, from a frozen corpus of 116 approved works;
- the **locator** — chapter, verse, mantra or sūtra — and which edition's
  numbering that locator follows;
- the **witness used**: the source actually opened and read;
- the **edition anchor**: the printed edition the text is tied to, which may be
  registered without having been consulted directly;
- the **translation status**, since the project's own renderings are working
  translations rather than published ones.

Where those chains are thin, the passage says so. Digital texts of the
Upaniṣads, for instance, frequently share a single transcription ancestry, so
agreement between two online versions is not independent attestation — and the
project will not present it as such.

A text with no reliable source is marked as a gap and stays unpublished. There
are currently sixteen of those in the corpus, and they are recorded rather than
quietly avoided.

</details>

## What this project will not do

It will not turn a resemblance into an influence, a text into a scientific
result, or a historical medical theory into medical advice.

It will not collapse disagreeing commentators into a single "Indian view". Where
schools differ, the difference is the content.

It will not use priority claims — first, earliest, invented, discovered — unless
the historical scholarship genuinely supports them, which is rarer than popular
writing suggests.

And it will not omit difficult material because it is difficult. Controversial
passages are quoted accurately, given their context, and distinguished from
endorsement.

## Rigour and wonder

The editorial standard the project is written against puts it plainly: rigour
should not remove wonder. If an entry is accurate but dull, the framing needs
work. If it is exciting but unsupported, it gets weakened or dropped.

Both failures are real. Only one of them misleads anybody.

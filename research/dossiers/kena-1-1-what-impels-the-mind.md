# Research Question

What makes the mind able to think?

More precisely, as the text puts it: when the mind goes out towards an object,
what sends it? The Kena Upanishad opens not with a doctrine but with an
interrogative, and its first word — *kena*, "by whom" / "by what" — is what
the text is named after.

# Why This Might Be Interesting

Reader reaction: **they asked this**.

The interesting thing is not the answer the text goes on to give. It is that
the question was posed at all, and posed in this shape: not "what is the
mind?" but "what stands behind the mind's *movement*?" The text treats the
mind as something driven rather than as the driver — it is grouped with
breath, speech, eye and ear as one more faculty that has to be accounted for,
not as the accounting principle. That regress-style move is the hook.

A secondary hook: the Upanishad is named after its own first word, so the
question *is* the title.

Text as read (IAST, from the primary witness):

> oṃ keneṣitaṃ patati preṣitaṃ manaḥ
> kena prāṇaḥ prathamaḥ praiti yuktaḥ |
> keneṣitāṃ vācamimāṃ vadanti
> cakṣuḥ śrotraṃ ka u devo yunakti ||

Word-level working gloss (mine, not a published translation):

- *kena* — by whom / by what (instrumental of *ka*)
- *iṣitam* — impelled, set in motion, willed
- *patati* — falls, flies, alights (on its object)
- *preṣitam* — sent forth, despatched
- *manaḥ* — mind
- *prāṇaḥ prathamaḥ* — the foremost breath
- *praiti yuktaḥ* — goes forth, yoked/harnessed
- *vācam imām vadanti* — they speak this speech
- *cakṣuḥ śrotram* — eye and ear
- *ka u devaḥ yunakti* — which god yokes (them)?

Note the vocabulary: *iṣita*, *preṣita*, *yukta*, *yunakti* are all verbs of
**sending and harnessing**. The mantra is built on a chariot/yoking metaphor,
not on a metaphor of illumination. An entry that renders this as "what lights
up the mind?" has substituted a different image.

A second candidate passage, if the entry needs the text's own reply rather
than only its question: Kena 1.2 (*śrotrasya śrotram manaso manaḥ* — "the ear
of the ear, the mind of the mind"). Would be `kena-1-2`. Not required for the
hook; the question alone carries an entry.

## Source / Edition Notes

- `sr-kena-vedicheritage` — primary witness, registered.
- `sr-kena-limaye-anchor` — printed edition anchor: **Limaye & Vadekar,
  *Eighteen Principal Upaniṣads* (1958)**. This is the anchor for most of the
  Upanishad records in this corpus.
- `sr-kena-gretil` — registered, but marked **derivative** (transcription
  lineage from TITUS). It is *not* an independent witness. Agreement between
  the GRETIL text and other digital Upanishad texts usually demonstrates

  textual independence.

  **Consequence:** do not write "all witnesses agree" as if that were
  independent corroboration. For this mantra we effectively have one printed
  edition standing behind the digital texts.

- Corpus `source_status` for `kena-upanishad` is **partially-verified**, not

  is not blocked and an entry built on it is not gated. But it is not a

- Embedding: the corpus record has `embedded_in: jaiminiya-upanishad-brahmana`

# Traditional Perspectives

Attributed, not merged.

- **Śaṅkara (Advaita).** Two commentaries survive under his name on this text —
  a *padabhāṣya* (word-by-word) and a *vākyabhāṣya* (sentence-level). That
  duplication is itself notable and is occasionally used in the scholarship to
  argue about authorship. Śaṅkara's reading: the question is rhetorical in
  structure but real in function — it drives the student to see that the
  faculties are instruments, and that the impeller is *ātman/brahman*, which
  cannot itself be an object of those instruments.
- **Madhva (Dvaita)** also commented on the Kena; the wisdomlib text of the
  Madhva commentary is available. His reading does not make the impeller
  identical with the self of the questioner in the Advaitin sense. **I have
  not yet read enough of Madhva's actual commentary on 1.1 to characterise it
  responsibly** — see Open Questions. Do not attribute a position to Madhva in
  a public entry on the strength of what is written here.
- **Later Vedāntic reception generally** treats 1.1 as the *praśna* (question)
  and 1.2–1.8 as the *uttara* (answer). This structural reading is uncontested
  across schools even where the content of the answer is disputed.

# Scholarly Perspectives

- Patrick Olivelle, *The Early Upaniṣads* (1998) / *Upaniṣads* (OWC), places
  the Kena among the **verse Upaniṣads** (with Kaṭha, Īśā, Muṇḍaka) — a group
  generally taken as later than the older prose Upaniṣads (Bṛhadāraṇyaka,
  Chāndogya). I have this from secondary reporting of Olivelle's chronology,
  not from the printed page. Confidence: moderate. Do not put a date range in
  an entry on this basis.
- The Kena's first section (verse) and its later sections (prose, containing
  the Umā Haimavatī narrative) are stylistically distinct, and the text is
  often discussed as composite. Relevant because an entry that says "the Kena
  Upanishad says X" is generalising across sections that may not be of a
  piece. Safer to say "the opening section of the Kena".
- **I did not find scholarly discussion specifically arguing about whether
  rendering this mantra in terms of "consciousness" is anachronistic.** That
  was the thing I went looking for and did not find. The caution below is
  therefore my own editorial judgement, flagged as EDITORIAL, not a reported
  scholarly position.

# Modern Connection

Tempting and dangerous.

The mantra can be made to sound like a statement of the "hard problem of
consciousness", or like a homunculus regress from philosophy of mind. Both
comparisons are available and both are *shape* resemblances, not content
resemblances.

If a connection is used at all, the honest one is narrow:

- **Relationship type:** structural parallel in the *form of the question*
  (a regress argument about faculties), not in its subject matter or method.
- **Where it stops:** the Upanishad is not asking a question about mechanism,
  is not proposing an explanation that competes with a physical one, and is
  not making an epistemic claim of the kind cognitive science makes. It moves
  immediately to a theological answer (*brahman*) that the modern question
  does not entertain. The parallel survives exactly one sentence and then
  breaks.

My recommendation: **leave `## Connect` out of this entry.** The question is
interesting enough unassisted, and every version of this comparison I can draft
edges towards "the ancients anticipated neuroscience", which is precisely what
`CLAIM_POLICY.md` exists to prevent.

# Claim Audit

**Claim 1 — The Kena Upanishad opens by asking what impels the mind.**
- Class: TEXTUAL
- Evidence: `sr-kena-vedicheritage` at 1.1; text quoted above; anchored to
  Limaye & Vadekar 1958 via `sr-kena-limaye-anchor`.
- Confidence: high
- Caveat: only one printed edition effectively stands behind the digital
  witnesses; `source_status` is partially-verified.
- Safe wording: "The Kena Upaniṣad opens with a question: impelled by what
  does the mind go out towards its object?"

**Claim 2 — The text is named after its own first word, *kena*.**
- Class: TEXTUAL
- Evidence: the mantra itself; standard and uncontested.
- Confidence: high
- Caveat: none material.
- Safe wording: "The text takes its name from its first word, *kena* — 'by
  what?'"

**Claim 3 — The mantra's verbs are verbs of sending and yoking.**
- Class: TEXTUAL (philological)
- Evidence: *iṣita*, *preṣita*, *yukta*, *yunakti* in the quoted text; standard
  lexical values.
- Confidence: high
- Caveat: this is my reading of the metaphor's register, which is an editorial
  layer on top of the lexicon. The lexical values are not in dispute; the claim
  that they constitute a sustained chariot image is a light interpretive step.
- Safe wording: "The mantra's verbs are verbs of impelling and harnessing —
  the mind is described as something *sent*, not as the sender."

**Claim 4 — Śaṅkara commented on this text, and two commentaries survive
under his name.**
- Class: COMMENTARIAL / HISTORICAL
- Evidence: the *padabhāṣya* and *vākyabhāṣya* are both transmitted as
  Śaṅkara's; widely reported.
- Confidence: moderate-to-high for the existence of both; **low** for
  Śaṅkara's authorship of both.
- Caveat: authorship of the pair is a live scholarly question. I have not read
  the argument.
- Safe wording: "Two commentaries on the Kena are transmitted under Śaṅkara's
  name." Do **not** write "Śaṅkara wrote two commentaries on the Kena."

**Claim 5 — The Kena is transmitted within the Jaiminīya Upaniṣad Brāhmaṇa.**
- Class: HISTORICAL
- Evidence: corpus record `embedded_in: jaiminiya-upanishad-brahmana`.
- Confidence: high for the fact of embedding; **none** for any specific
  locator within the JUB.
- Caveat: `range: null` in the corpus record. No JUB chapter/verse may be
  cited.
- Safe wording: "The Kena is transmitted as part of the Jaiminīya Upaniṣad
  Brāhmaṇa, which is why it is also called the Talavakāra Upaniṣad."

**Claim 6 — Reading the mantra as a question about "consciousness" imports a
modern category.**
- Class: EDITORIAL
- Evidence: none external. This is my judgement, based on the mantra's own
  vocabulary (impulsion/yoking, not awareness/illumination) and on the absence
  of a term in the mantra that maps onto "consciousness".
- Confidence: moderate, and *as an editorial caution* rather than as a claim
  about the text.
- Caveat: I looked for scholarly treatment of exactly this point and did not
  find it. Do not present it as the scholarly consensus.
- Safe wording: leave it out of the entry body; let the translation do the
  work by rendering *iṣita/preṣita* as impulsion rather than as awareness.

# Counterevidence / Disagreement

- **The question may be rhetorical, not open.** On the traditional reading the
  Upanishad already knows the answer and stages the question pedagogically. An
  entry framed as "they asked a question we still ask" risks implying an
  open-ended inquiry the text does not conduct. This is the strongest argument
  against the hook as I have drafted it.
- **The mantra may be less about the mind than my framing suggests.** *manaḥ*
  is the first item in a list — breath, speech, eye, ear follow, and the final
  clause (*ka u devo yunakti*) is about the whole set. Singling out the mind is
  an editorial choice, and a reader who knows the text could fairly say the
  mantra is about the faculties collectively, with mind merely listed first.
  The entry should not pretend the mantra is *about the mind* specifically.
- **"Composite text" cuts against generalisation.** Whatever the verse section
  is doing, the prose Umā narrative later in the text is doing something else.
- **The chariot-metaphor reading is contestable.** *yuj* is common enough in
  this register that a reader might hear nothing specifically chariot-like.

# What We Should Not Say

- ❌ "The Kena Upanishad asks the hard problem of consciousness."
  Imports a 1995 formulation into a text that is not asking about mechanism.
- ❌ "Ancient Indians had already discovered that the mind is not the self."
  Priority framing; also flattens a contested doctrinal claim into a discovery.
- ❌ "All the manuscripts agree on this verse."
  We have not consulted manuscripts. We have consulted digital witnesses with
  shared transcription lineage behind one printed edition.
- ❌ "Kena 1.1 corresponds to Jaiminīya Upaniṣad Brāhmaṇa [any locator]."
  The range is unrecorded. This would be fabricated.
- ❌ "Śaṅkara wrote two commentaries on this text."
  Say "transmitted under his name".
- ❌ "This is the oldest question about the mind in world literature."
  Unverifiable, and a priority claim.
- ❌ Translating *keneṣitaṃ patati manaḥ* as "what makes the mind conscious".
  Not what the words say.

# Entry Direction

- **Hook (Discover):** the text is named after the word "by what?" — it opens
  with a question rather than a teaching, and the question is about something
  we normally treat as the questioner.
- **Passage carrying it:** `kena-1-1`, transcluded into `## Original`. Passage
  note must be created first.
- **Understand (~140 words):** the grammar of impulsion; the mind listed
  alongside breath, speech, eye, ear; the regress that generates the question.
  Do not give the answer here.
- **Perspectives:** Śaṅkara's reading of question-as-pedagogy; note that the
  text answers itself at 1.2 and that schools diverge on what the answer
  means. Attribute; do not merge.
- **Connect:** recommend omitting (see Modern Connection).
- **Explore:** Read → `kena-1-2` (the answer). Understand → a concept note on
  *manas*, if one exists. Perspective → Śaṅkara. Compare → Kaṭha's chariot
  simile, which uses the same yoking vocabulary on the same problem. Listen →
  recitation via `sr-kena-vedicheritage` if the record carries audio.

Shape note: this entry works best as a **short** entry. The temptation is to
resolve the question; the entry is stronger if it stops at the question and
sends the reader onward.

## Revised at conversion — 2026-09-22

The entry was built as `what-makes-the-mind-able-to-think`, on passage
`kena-1-1`. Two things in the plan above changed, both tightening it.

**Named commentary is omitted entirely.** The plan above proposed a
Perspectives section built on Shankara, with Madhva deferred. On inspection
of `data/sources.yaml`, **all three registered Kena source records carry
`provides_commentary: false`** — `sr-kena-vedicheritage`, `sr-kena-gretil`
and `sr-kena-limaye-anchor` are text witnesses only. There is no registered
commentary source for this text at all.

So the choice was not between Shankara and Madhva; it was between an
unsourced summary and an honest absence. The entry says plainly that
commentary is being left out, and why. That is a better first prototype than
a Perspectives section resting on second-hand attribution, and it also
demonstrates the failure mode the project is built to avoid.

This supersedes the Perspectives line in **Entry Direction** above. The
Shankara material in **Traditional Perspectives** stays in this dossier as
research, unused, pending a registered commentary source.

**`## Connect` is included after all**, against the recommendation above,
because the brief called for it and the restrained version survives
inspection. It is typed `still-the-same-question`, claims only that the
question has proved durable and has the same self-referential awkwardness
now, and states explicitly that the verse is not doing neuroscience, that
nothing in it corresponds to a scientific result, and that "consciousness"
is a modern category not present in the Sanskrit. The recommendation above
was the safer call; the version written is the narrower one it asked for.

**Not changed:** the passage carries no answer, only the question; the mind
is one item in a list and the entry says so; no JUB coordinate is cited; no
date appears anywhere.

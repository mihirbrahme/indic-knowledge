# Research Question

How should a physician know what is wrong?

Not "what are the diseases" but the prior question: by what *means of
knowing* does a physician establish the condition in front of them? The
Caraka Saṃhitā asks this explicitly and answers it with a short, closed list.

# Why This Might Be Interesting

Reader reaction: **they asked this**, with a strong secondary **I
misunderstood this**.

Two things make it interesting.

First, the answer is **epistemological, not clinical**. The text does not
open by listing symptoms; it opens by naming the *instruments of knowledge*
available to the physician, and it names three of them. That is a
philosophical move — the same *pramāṇa* vocabulary the darśana literature
uses — appearing inside a medical treatise, because the medical problem is
treated as a problem of knowing.

Second, the list includes **testimony first**. A modern reader expects
observation to head the list, or to be the whole list. Caraka puts
*āptopadeśa* — authoritative teaching — before *pratyakṣa* (direct
perception) and *anumāna* (inference). That ordering is the thing worth
sitting with, and it is where readers most often misread the text by assuming
it must mean something more familiar than it does.

# Primary Text Evidence

## Passage Candidates

- **Corpus text:** `charaka-samhita`
- **Locator:** Vimānasthāna 4.3, under the corpus
  `citation_scheme: [sthana, adhyaya, verse]`.
- **Chapter:** *Trividha Roga Viśeṣa Vijñānīya Vimāna* — "the chapter on the
  threefold means of specific knowledge of disease". The chapter is *named*
  after this teaching, which strengthens the case that 4.3 is the right
  anchor and not an incidental remark.
- **Locator follows:** `sr-caraka-sarit`.
- **Passage note:** does **not** exist. Would be `caraka-vimanasthana-4-3`.

Text as read (IAST/ISO, from the primary witness):

> trividhaṃ khalu rogaviśeṣavijñānaṃ bhavati — tadyathā āptopadeśaḥ
> pratyakṣam anumānaṃ ca

Working translation (mine, flat):

> The specific knowledge of disease is indeed of three kinds: namely,
> authoritative teaching, direct perception, and inference.

Terms:

- *āptopadeśa* — the teaching of a reliable authority (*āpta*, one who is
  trustworthy because they know and do not deceive)
- *pratyakṣa* — direct perception, what the physician themselves observes
- *anumāna* — inference, reasoning to what is not directly observable
- *rogaviśeṣavijñāna* — knowing the *specific* disease; note *viśeṣa*, the
  discriminating particular, not disease in general

**Caveat on the locator.** The corpus record carries
`citation_scheme_review: true`. Verse numbering within an adhyāya varies
between editions of the Caraka Saṃhitā, particularly across prose passages
where the segmentation into numbered units is an editorial act. The
*sthāna* and *adhyāya* (Vimānasthāna 4) are secure; the *.3* is the soft part.
The chapter title provides a fallback identification that does not depend on
the verse number.

**Adjacent material worth checking before drafting:** Vimānasthāna 8 contains
the extended treatment of examination of the patient, and is where the
much-cited enumerations of examination methods sit. I have **not** verified
its content in this pass. Do not conflate the threefold *pramāṇa* list at 4.3
with any longer examination list; they are different teachings and merging
them is a common secondary-literature error.

- Corpus `source_status`: **verified**. The text and this passage are securely

- **Compositional layering.** The Caraka Saṃhitā is traditionally described as

  Cikitsāsthāna and the last two sthānas, which would leave Vimānasthāna in
  the older stratum, but **I have not verified this and it must not be stated
  as fact**.

# Traditional Perspectives

- **Cakrapāṇidatta**, *Āyurveda Dīpikā* (11th c.) is the standard commentary
  on the Caraka Saṃhitā and is printed alongside the Trikamjī text. He is the
  authority to consult on how *āptopadeśa* is delimited — in particular, on
  the question of what makes someone *āpta*, and whether the category is
  restricted to the received medical tradition or extends more broadly.
  **I have not read his gloss on this passage.** Nothing may be attributed to
  him yet.
- **The Nyāya connection.** The threefold list is the Nyāya *pramāṇa* scheme
  minus *upamāna* (comparison) — Nyāya classically has four. Caraka elsewhere
  discusses *yukti* (reasoned application) as a further means, which is one of
  the distinctive Āyurvedic contributions to *pramāṇa* theory and a point of
  disagreement with the Naiyāyikas. **I have not verified where Caraka
  discusses *yukti*, or how it relates to the threefold list at 4.3.** This is
  potentially the most interesting thread in the whole dossier and it is
  currently unresearched.
- Do not merge "the Āyurvedic view" with "the Nyāya view". They share
  vocabulary and disagree about the inventory.

# Scholarly Perspectives

- The Caraka Saṃhitā is standardly read as a text with substantial
  philosophical content, not merely a pharmacopoeia — the Vimānasthāna and
  Sūtrasthāna contain sustained discussion of debate, definition, causation
  and means of knowledge. The epistemological opening of Vimānasthāna 4 is an
  instance of this, not an anomaly.
- Dating is contested and the layered composition makes a single date
  meaningless. **No date should appear publicly** from this dossier.
- The relationship between Āyurvedic *pramāṇa* discussion and the darśana
  schools — who borrowed from whom, and whether the Caraka discussions predate
  the systematised Nyāya — is an active scholarly question. This project takes
  no position.
- **I did not, in this pass, consult scholarly literature directly on
  Vimānasthāna 4.3.** The characterisations above are general. Treat the
  Scholarly Perspectives section of any resulting entry as unresearched until
  this is fixed.

# Modern Connection

**Optional, and I recommend it be handled with more restraint than usual.**

There is a genuine and narrow structural observation available:

- **Relationship type:** structural parallel in *how the problem is framed* —
  the text treats "how do I know what is wrong?" as a question about sources
  of knowledge, and distinguishes what one is told, what one observes, and
  what one infers.
- **Where it stops — and it stops hard:**
  1. **Conceptual comparison is not clinical validation.** Noticing that a
     text distinguishes testimony, observation and inference says nothing
     whatever about whether any Āyurvedic diagnostic or therapeutic practice
     works.
  2. **Historical medical theory is not present-day medical advice.** The
     Caraka Saṃhitā is a historical document. Describing its content is not
     recommending its practice.
  3. The three *pramāṇas* are categories in a Sanskrit epistemological
     vocabulary with their own commitments. *āptopadeśa* in particular is not
     "consulting the literature" — it rests on a notion of the reliable person
     that has no modern counterpart.
  4. Any sentence that makes the text sound like it anticipated a modern
     clinical or scientific method is out of scope.

If the restraint above cannot be preserved in the entry's own voice, omit
`## Connect` entirely.

# Claim Audit

**Claim 1 — The Caraka Saṃhitā states that specific knowledge of disease is
of three kinds: authoritative teaching, direct perception, and inference.**
- Class: TEXTUAL
- Evidence: *trividhaṃ khalu rogaviśeṣavijñānaṃ bhavati — tadyathā
  āptopadeśaḥ pratyakṣam anumānaṃ ca*, Vimānasthāna 4, via `sr-caraka-sarit`;
  edition anchor `sr-caraka-trikamji-anchor`; chapter title corroborates.
- Confidence: high for the content; **moderate for the verse number**
  (`citation_scheme_review: true`).
- Caveat: digital witnesses share Trikamjī ancestry; this is one edition, not
  independent attestation.
- Safe wording: "In the Vimānasthāna, the Caraka Saṃhitā says that specific
  knowledge of a disease comes about in three ways: authoritative teaching,
  direct perception, and inference." Prefer chapter-level citation if the
  verse number cannot be confirmed.

**Claim 2 — The chapter is named after this teaching.**
- Class: TEXTUAL
- Evidence: chapter title *Trividha Roga Viśeṣa Vijñānīya Vimāna*.
- Confidence: high
- Caveat: chapter titles are transmitted in the text but are worth confirming
  against the Trikamjī edition's own table of contents.
- Safe wording: "The chapter takes its name from this threefold division."

**Claim 3 — Testimony (*āptopadeśa*) is listed first.**
- Class: TEXTUAL
- Evidence: the word order in the quoted sūtra.
- Confidence: high for the order as transmitted.
- Caveat: **word order in a Sanskrit list does not automatically encode
  priority or rank.** The claim that listing it first *means* it ranks first
  is an interpretive step, and I have no commentarial support for it. This is
  the single biggest risk in the entry as I have framed it.
- Safe wording: "The list begins with authoritative teaching." Do **not**
  write "Caraka ranks testimony above observation" without Cakrapāṇidatta or
  comparable support.

**Claim 4 — This is the Nyāya *pramāṇa* vocabulary, minus *upamāna*.**
- Class: EDITORIAL / HISTORICAL
- Evidence: the terms are shared; Nyāya's classical four are *pratyakṣa,
  anumāna, upamāna, śabda*.
- Confidence: high that the vocabulary is shared; **low** as to any claim of
  direction of influence, dependence, or chronological relation.
- Caveat: shared vocabulary is not borrowing. Both may draw on a common
  intellectual milieu.
- Safe wording: "The three terms belong to the same vocabulary of *means of
  knowledge* used in the philosophical schools." Do not say Caraka "adopted"
  or "borrowed" the scheme.

**Claim 5 — The Caraka Saṃhitā is a layered text (Agniveśa / Caraka /
Dṛḍhabala).**
- Class: HISTORICAL
- Evidence: standard and traditionally stated within the text's own framing.
- Confidence: high for the layering; **unverified** as to which layer
  Vimānasthāna 4 belongs to.
- Caveat: do not assign this passage to a stratum.
- Safe wording: "The Caraka Saṃhitā as we have it is a layered work,
  traditionally described as Agniveśa's treatise redacted by Caraka and later
  supplemented by Dṛḍhabala."

**Claim 6 — Anything about whether Āyurvedic diagnosis works.**

- Evidence: none, and none will be sought here.
- Confidence: n/a
- Caveat: this project describes texts. It does not evaluate therapies, in

- Safe wording: silence, plus the explicit statement that historical medical

# Counterevidence / Disagreement

- **The "testimony first" hook may be reading rank into a list.** Sanskrit
  enumerations frequently order by convention, metre, or expository
  convenience. If Cakrapāṇidatta does not treat the order as significant, the
  hook loses most of its force and the entry needs reframing. This is the
  central vulnerability.
- **The threefold list may not be the text's operative method.** Vimānasthāna
  8 and the Sūtrasthāna contain much more concrete examination material. A
  specialist could reasonably say the physician's actual epistemic practice in
  Caraka is richer than a three-item list, and that foregrounding 4.3 gives a
  misleadingly tidy picture.
- **The "philosophical medicine" framing may be overdrawn.** Presenting Caraka
  as unusual for containing epistemology risks implying that medical texts
  elsewhere did not — a comparative claim I have not checked.
- **"Physician" may be the wrong frame.** The passage says knowledge *of
  disease* is threefold; it does not obviously restrict itself to the
  physician's activity. My research question may have narrowed the text.
- **The Suśruta Saṃhitā** handles the same problem differently. An entry that
  presents Caraka's answer as *the* Āyurvedic answer overstates it. Check
  whether Suśruta is in Corpus v1.

# What We Should Not Say

- ❌ Anything that reads as medical advice, or as a suggestion that a reader
  should use, try, or rely on any practice described. **Historical medical
  theory is not present-day medical advice.**
- ❌ "Āyurveda anticipated evidence-based medicine" / "...the scientific
  method" / "...modern diagnostics."
  **Conceptual comparison is not clinical validation.**
- ❌ "Caraka proved / demonstrated / showed that..."
  The text states; it does not demonstrate in that sense.
- ❌ "Caraka ranked testimony above observation."
  Word order is not rank without commentarial support.
- ❌ "Ayurvedic diagnosis is scientifically validated" — and equally,
  "Ayurvedic diagnosis has been debunked." Both are out of scope; this
  project describes the text.
- ❌ "Caraka wrote..." as a flat attribution.
  Layered text; say "the Caraka Saṃhitā says".
- ❌ "Caraka borrowed the *pramāṇa* scheme from Nyāya" (or the reverse).
  Direction of influence is unestablished.
- ❌ "Caraka Saṃhitā, Vimānasthāna 4.3" stated with confidence, if the verse
  number cannot be confirmed against Trikamjī. Cite the chapter instead.
- ❌ Any date for the text.
- ❌ Naming *yukti* as a fourth means of knowledge. Not yet located.

# Entry Direction

- **Hook (Discover):** a medical treatise opens its chapter on identifying
  disease not with symptoms but with a question about knowledge — and its
  answer begins with what you are *told*.
- **Passage carrying it:** `caraka-vimanasthana-4-3`, transcluded into
  `## Original`. Passage note must be created first, with
  `citation_review: provisional` given the numbering caveat.
- **Understand (~170 words):** what the three terms mean; what *āpta* means
  and why it is not "the literature"; that *viśeṣa* means the specific, the
  discriminated particular, so this is about telling *this* condition from a
  similar one. State plainly that the text is historical.
- **Perspectives:** Cakrapāṇidatta on *āptopadeśa* (once read); the shared
  vocabulary with the darśanas, without a direction-of-influence claim.
  Attribute; do not merge.
